# ISSUE 4.5 — "THE SHAPE THAT KEPT SHOWING UP"
### TARTAN — trajectories recursively tiled and reused across scales
### Page-by-page poster format, matching Issues 1–4

**Case type:** Starts as an accusation of theft, resolves into something
stranger than theft — the same pattern turning up honestly, independently,
at every scale, because it's not a design choice at all but a structural
optimum. Third issue in the series (after 2.5) built around "there is no
villain," but this time the surprise isn't absence of fault — it's the
presence of the same shape everywhere you didn't expect to find it.

**The loss — or rather, the accusation:** A small town's traffic engineer
files a complaint: the state's new interstate interchange uses the "exact"
shape of her town's little traffic circle, blown up a hundredfold. She wants
credit. The state engineer swears he's never seen her design.

**This issue's device — the zoom, not a taxonomy:** rather than categories,
this issue is built around a single recursive image, the same shape nested
inside itself at four scales:

1. A single block's pedestrian crossing pattern (Page 1)
2. The town's traffic circle (Page 3, first zoom level)
3. The city's ring road (Page 3, second zoom level)
4. The interstate interchange (Page 4/5, the accused design)

Do not caption the recurrence until Page 6 — the reader should notice the
same shape nesting inside itself before anyone in the story says so out
loud.

---

## PAGE 1 — THE MYSTERY

**Main art:** A courthouse steps. A small-town engineer, blueprints under
her arm, facing a state engineer and a lawyer. Behind them both, faint
overlaid in the sky like a watermark: the same circular interchange shape,
small.

**Panel A:** The town engineer — *"That's my circle. I designed it in 1987
for six streets. You didn't even change the angles."*
**Panel B:** The state engineer, genuinely baffled — *"I've never been to
your town. I designed this for eleven lanes of interstate."*
**Panel C:** Close on the blueprints, side by side — impossible to tell
which is which without the scale bar.

**Bottom banner:** CASE FILE #4.5: THE SAME SHAPE, TWICE, WITHOUT AN
EXPLANATION

---

## PAGE 2 — THE ACCUSATION

**Panel A:** The town engineer's original 1987 circle — six streets meeting
at even intervals, a small rounded island in the middle.
**Panel B:** The interstate interchange — the identical geometry, scaled up,
eleven lanes instead of six streets.
**Center caption:**
> One of these took an afternoon to sketch. One of these took eleven years
> and forty million dollars. They are, geometrically, the same drawing.

**Bottom banner:** SOMEBODY MUST HAVE COPIED SOMEBODY. THAT'S THE ONLY WAY
THIS HAPPENS. RIGHT?

---

## PAGE 3 — THE ZOOM

**Full page, visual centerpiece.** A single image, four concentric layers,
each one the same rounded-junction shape, nested inside the next like
tree rings: pedestrian crossing at the center, the town's traffic circle
around it, the city's ring road around that, the interstate interchange as
the outermost ring. The Detective stands at the very center, small, looking
outward through all four layers at once.

**Sidebar, not a taxonomy this time — a single caption ladder:**
> Same shape at the scale of a sidewalk.
> Same shape at the scale of a town.
> Same shape at the scale of a city.
> Same shape at the scale of a state.

**Bottom caption:**
> Nobody plans four things at once to look like this.
> Something about the shape itself keeps getting rediscovered.

---

## PAGE 4 — THE INVESTIGATION BOARD

**Full page,** corkboard — but the pins connect not two suspects, but six
unrelated locations across three centuries, each with the same rounded-
junction shape independently documented: a Roman crossroads, a medieval
village green, a 1950s cul-de-sac, the town's 1987 circle, the city ring
road, the interstate interchange.

**Evidence card, center, large:**
INDEPENDENT DISCOVERY, NOT COPYING
Caption: *No route connects any of these designers to any of the others.
Centuries and oceans apart. Same shape, every time, wherever the same
problem showed up: merge several flows as cheaply as possible.*

**Bottom banner:** IT WASN'T STOLEN FROM ANYONE. IT WAS FOUND BY EVERYONE
WHO ASKED THE SAME QUESTION.

---

## PAGE 5 — WALKING THE TERRITORY

**Large vertical panel:** The Detective at a drafting table, physically
overlaying tracing paper of all four designs — sidewalk, town circle, ring
road, interchange — on top of each other, adjusting only the scale bar.
They align perfectly, layer over layer.

**Small insets:**
Town engineer's claim: "I designed this."
State engineer's claim: "I designed this independently."
Both: ✔ true.

**Final panel:** The Detective holding all four tracings up to the light at
once, the shape glowing through every layer.
> Caption: The rule wasn't copied from a drawing. It was copied from the
> problem. Anyone who solved the same problem honestly would land here.

---

## PAGE 6 — THE CONFRONTATION (WITHOUT AN ADVERSARY)

**Panel A:** The town engineer, blueprints lowered, no longer angry —
studying the nested-rings diagram from Page 3 instead.
**Panel B:** The state engineer, same posture, same diagram.
**Panel C:** Both of them, together now, looking at the same page instead
of at each other.

**Visual centerpiece:** the four nested rings from Page 3, now fully
labeled for the first time — the reader's private discovery made public
inside the story.

**Detective:**
> "Neither of you found this shape. You both rediscovered it. It shows up
> wherever the cheapest way to merge four flows is the same cheapest way,
> whether that's six streets or eleven lanes. You weren't rivals. You were
> the same experiment, run twice."

## PAGE 7 — THE FRAMEWORK (manifesto page)

**Large central figure:** The Detective, the four nested rings behind him
now rendered as a single glowing spiral rather than separate layers — the
recursive structure made explicit.

**No icon row this issue — instead, one large diagram:** a small road
pattern, and beside it, the identical pattern reappearing at three
increasing scales, each labeled only with a magnification number (×1, ×10,
×100).

**Giant text:** TRAJECTORIES DO NOT JUST HAPPEN ONCE. THE SAME SHAPE CAN BE
TILED ACROSS EVERY SCALE THAT ASKS THE SAME QUESTION.

**Small line, forward gesture into Issue 5:**
> The shape kept winning at every scale. That raises a harder question:
> winning according to what? A pattern this persistent isn't just efficient
> — something is pulling every scale toward it. That pull is next.

**Bottom strip:** the five domain icons again, now each shown as a small
spiral of self-similar shapes rather than a single image — signaling this
recursive-tiling question applies as much to institutions and language as
to roads.

## PAGE 8 — RESOLUTION

**Top panel:** The courthouse steps again, but now both engineers signing a
joint credit line instead of dueling claims — "Independently and jointly
discovered."

**Middle panel:** A plaque proposed for the interchange, crediting not a
person but the shape itself, with a small diagram of the four nested rings.

**Large final panel:** The Lighthouse, its spiral fresnel lens for once
doing double duty as the recursive-ring motif — the same spiral shape
appearing in the lighthouse lens as in the roads below it.

**Final caption:**
> Nothing here was stolen. The same good idea just kept being the cheapest
> way out, at every size anyone tried it.

**Footer:** IT WASN'T COPIED. IT WAS DISCOVERED, REPEATEDLY, BY EVERYONE WHO
ASKED THE RIGHT QUESTION.

## Production notes

- The nested-rings image on Page 3 is the issue's signature and should be
  held back from full clarity until Page 6 — Page 3 shows it as pure
  geometry, uncaptioned by scale; only Page 6 puts the labels on. That
  delay is what makes the "wait, that's the same shape four times" landing
  feel like the reader's own discovery rather than something explained to
  them.
- This issue's cold open plays the accusation completely straight — the
  reader should genuinely suspect one engineer stole from the other through
  Pages 1–2. The turn on Page 4 (independent discovery across centuries)
  needs to land as a real surprise, not a foregone structural beat, even
  though returning readers may now expect "no villain" issues from CLIO.
  Undercutting that expectation once (playing the accusation straight
  longer than usual) keeps the formula from calcifying.
- Page 7's forward line is deliberately more pointed than Issue 2.5's —
  it names the open question (what is the shape optimizing toward) rather
  than just gesturing at a future topic, since Preference Fields (Issue 5)
  needs a sharper hook than "here's what comes next."
